HTA annual report 2025 to 2026 and the version you cite

27 October 2026 · By Pro-curo team · Reviewed by Mark Walker · 6 min read

Why this matters

  • The HTA added a Welsh translation and a web accessible PDF of its 2025 to 2026 annual report and accounts on 20 October 2026.
  • The two English PDFs share one ISBN and one HC number but run to 84 and 82 pages, so page references may not match between them.
  • Any team that cites regulator documents in procedures, governance papers or inspection packs faces the same version and traceability question.

The Human Tissue Authority (HTA) has updated the GOV.UK page for its annual report and accounts for 2025 to 2026, which covers the period 1 April 2025 to 31 March 2026. The report was first published on 16 July 2026. On 20 October 2026 the HTA added a Welsh translation and a web accessible PDF of the English language version.

The update is small, but it carries a clear operational lesson for anyone who cites regulator documents in quality records, inspection preparation packs or governance papers. One report now exists in several formats, and the English formats do not share the same page count.

A note on scope. This article draws on the GOV.UK publication page, not the full text of the report. The page lists what the report contains but does not summarise its findings, so we do not report performance figures, governance conclusions or financial results here. Those are in the primary document, linked at the end.

What happened

According to the publication page, the report includes information about performance, the annual governance statement and the financial statements. It carries the reference ISBN 978-1-5286-6260-4 and the paper number HC 460.

The original English PDF is a 3.9 MB file of 84 pages. The page carries a warning about that file: "This file may not be suitable for users of assistive technology," as the GOV.UK page puts it. The page also sets out a route to request an accessible format by email to publications@dhsc.gov.uk, asking the requester to say what format they need and what assistive technology they use.

The web accessible version added on 20 October 2026 is a 4.31 MB file of 82 pages. It carries the same ISBN and the same HC number as the original. The page's update log records the Welsh translation as added on the same day.

The update log describes the October change as the addition of a translation and an accessible PDF. It does not describe any revision to the content of the report, and this article does not assume one. The reason for the two-page difference between the English files is not stated on the page.

Why it matters beyond the HTA

Regulator documents travel. A passage from an annual report can end up quoted in a local standard operating procedure, a governance committee paper, a training slide or an inspection self-assessment. When it does, the citation becomes part of the team's own record, and it needs to lead whoever checks it later to the same words.

The HTA page shows three ways a citation can drift, even when nothing in the underlying text has changed.

Page numbers differ between formats. The original English PDF has 84 pages and the web accessible PDF has 82. A reference that gives only a page number may not lead a reader to the same passage in both files.

Dates on the page mean different things. The page shows a first published date of 16 July 2026 and a last updated date of 20 October 2026. A record that notes only the later date could suggest the report itself was reissued in October, when the update log describes added formats.

One identifier covers several files. Both English PDFs carry ISBN 978-1-5286-6260-4 and HC 460. That is useful for identifying the document, but on its own it does not tell a reader which file a page reference came from.

None of this is a criticism of the HTA. Publishing accessible and Welsh versions adds options for readers. The point is that any organisation holding records that cite external documents faces the same issue, and the HTA's annual report is a timely, concrete example of it.

The general lesson

A document's identity is more than its title. For a citation to stay reliable, it generally needs enough detail for someone else to retrieve the same words: the title, the publisher, any reference number, the format or version, the date it was accessed, and a location within the document.

Sample custodians will recognise the principle. A sample record is only as useful as its history: where the material came from, which aliquot is which, who handled it and when. Documents behave in a similar way. The original English PDF, the web accessible PDF and the Welsh translation of the HTA report are, in effect, three renditions of one parent document, and a record that does not say which one was used loses part of its traceability.

There is a second, quieter lesson about accessibility. The HTA's original PDF carries a warning that it may not suit users of assistive technology, and a web accessible version was added later. Teams that publish their own documents, such as donor information sheets, reports to governance boards or local policies, may recognise the pattern of a document existing first in one format and later in others. Each new format is a point at which page references and version records can fall out of step.

A practical checklist

The items below are things teams typically review when a regulator document they rely on is updated. They are prompts for discussion, not a statement of what any organisation is required to do.

  • Which version of the HTA annual report is cited in local documents, and whether the citation records the format used (original PDF, web accessible PDF or Welsh translation).
  • Whether page references name the file they come from, given that the two English PDFs run to 84 and 82 pages.
  • Whether citations include the ISBN and HC number, which identify the report across its English formats.
  • Whether records distinguish the first published date (16 July 2026) from the page's last updated date (20 October 2026).
  • Whether downloaded copies of regulator documents held on shared drives are labelled with the source URL and the date downloaded.
  • Whether a named owner or shared mailbox receives GOV.UK email updates for the regulator pages the team relies on.
  • Whether staff who use assistive technology know the route, set out on the publication page, for requesting an accessible format.

How teams typically handle it

For the HTA report, the publication page provides the details needed for a fairly complete record of an external document: the title, the publisher (the Human Tissue Authority), the reference ISBN 978-1-5286-6260-4 and HC 460, and the format, file size and page count of each English PDF. A record of this kind can also note the source URL, the date accessed, the internal documents that cite it, and an owner responsible for watching for updates.

The HTA page itself shows that a publication page can change after it first appears: its update log records new files added on 20 October 2026, three months after first publication. For the HTA report, a record that notes which of the files on the page was downloaded, and when, keeps that history visible.

Another common approach is to cite by section rather than by page. The publication page names the main parts of the report: information about performance, the annual governance statement and the financial statements. A reference to a named section depends less on the file used than a bare page number does, and a page number can still be added with the format stated alongside it.

Alerts help with the watching. The GOV.UK page offers an option to get emails about this page. Routing alerts of this kind to a shared mailbox or a named role, rather than to one person's inbox, keeps them useful when staff change roles.

An update like the one on 20 October 2026 is a straightforward prompt to confirm that each citation of the report in local documents still leads to the intended passage.

Where to read the primary document

The full report, in both English formats, is on the HTA annual report and accounts 2025 to 2026 page on GOV.UK. According to its update log, the page also carries the Welsh translation, and it explains how to request an alternative accessible format.
